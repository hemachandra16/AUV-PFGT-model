Every frame below is from the held-out test set: the detector was never trained on any of them.

What you are looking at. Two copies of the same photograph. On the left, the boxes a human marked by hand — the correct answer. On the right, the boxes the software drew on its own. The closer the two match, the better.

What the headline score means. The detector scores mAP@0.5 = 0.829. That is not “83% correct” — it is a combined measure of how well it balances finding the animals against raising false alarms, averaged over all ten categories, counting a box as right if it overlaps the true one by at least half. Two plainer numbers from the same run: it finds about 76% of the animals that are actually there (recall), and about 84% of the boxes it draws are real (precision).

This page is deliberately not flattering. The frames were chosen to cover the three categories the detector handles best (cuttlefish, turtle, diver) and the three it handles worst (corals, scallop, sea cucumber) — so its weaknesses are on screen, not hidden.

One thing worth knowing

Running photos through the colour-correction software before showing them to this detector makes it worse, by about 3.9 points. That was measured on all 4,200 test frames, and it is why the tool detects on the raw photograph by default and only uses the corrected version for the picture a person looks at. This matches published findings from other groups — it is not unique to this system.
